The last eukaryotic common ancestor (LECA) is the hypothetical most recent common ancestor of all living eukaryotes – organisms whose cells have a nucleus, around 2 billion years ago. This followed the appearance some 3.5-4 billion years ago of the last universal common ancestor (LUCA), a much smaller cell which had the genetic code but no nucleus, and which gave rise to the Bacteria and the Archaea. The process by which the LECA came into being, eukaryogenesis, is not understood in detail, but is thought to have involved symbiogenesis, the coming together of an archaean and a bacterium which formed the cell's mitochondria.
The LECA's structure and function have been reconstructed by comparing the genomes of modern eukaryotes. This has led biologists to propose that the LECA was a complex cell with a nucleus with a nucleolus and eu/heterochromatin, an endoplasmic reticulum, peroxisomes, endo- and lysosomes, the ESCRT system, a Golgi apparatus, actin-based endo- and exocytosis, pseudopodia or filopodia, sterol-based membranes, G3P + ester bond phospholipids, a microtubule-based cytoskeleton with an organising centre, basal bodies, vacuoles, iron-sulphur cluster biosynthesis via the CIA system, mitochondria, and microtubule-based flagella.
The LECA had mechanisms for reshuffling its genetic material, and was likely able to reproduce sexually and repair damage to its DNA. It was likely able to detect and repair damage to its cell membrane, using mechanisms including calcium signalling and contraction of muscle-like actomyosin proteins.
